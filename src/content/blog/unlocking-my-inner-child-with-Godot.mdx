###

Ah, the 90s. A golden age of video games where chunky pixels reigned supreme, soundtracks were bleepy yet magical, and platformers were the king of genres. Growing up, I spent countless hours mastering the likes of Super Mario World, Sonic the Hedgehog, and Donkey Kong Country. These games didn’t just entertain me—they inspired me. And now, armed with Godot and a healthy dose of nostalgia, I’m making my very own 90s-style platformer.

If you’ve ever dreamt of creating your own retro-inspired game, let me take you on a light-hearted journey through my process. Spoiler alert: it’s a mix of joy, frustration, and pure geeky delight.

## Choosing the Tools (Hello, Godot!)

When it comes to game engines, the options can feel overwhelming. Unity? Unreal Engine? Something custom? I settled on Godot, and let me tell you why: it’s free, open-source, and incredibly beginner-friendly. Plus, it’s lightweight enough to run smoothly on my slightly ageing laptop—a crucial factor for someone who spent more on retro cartridges than modern hardware.

Godot’s 2D engine is a dream for pixel art games. The editor’s intuitive interface made me feel like I knew what I was doing (even when I didn’t), and its scripting language, GDScript, is close enough to Python to keep me from crying into my tea.

## Getting My Hands Dirty with Pixel Art

The first thing a 90s-inspired platformer needs is some proper pixel art. With dreams of 16-bit forests and caves in mind, I dusted off my copy of Aseprite, a pixel art tool that’s as charming as the sprites it helps you create. Designing my main character was both exciting and humbling—turns out, those old-school developers were really good at making things look amazing within a handful of pixels.

My protagonist? A plucky little robot named "Chipper" who’s on a quest to save the world from malfunctioning vending machines (a decidedly 90s-style plot, if I do say so myself). Once Chipper was animated with a jaunty walk cycle, I felt like a proud parent.

## Building Levels That Feel Right

Ah, level design—the heart and soul of any platformer. This was where I channelled my inner Shigeru Miyamoto, carefully laying out platforms, hazards, and secret areas. Godot’s TileMap system made this surprisingly fun. I created a tileset of dirt, grass, and bricks, then dragged and dropped them into place like digital Lego.

Of course, no 90s platformer is complete without collectibles. Enter the "Power Snacks," shiny little orbs scattered throughout the levels that Chipper can hoard. Adding them was as simple as creating a scene in Godot and instancing it across the map.

## Writing Code Without Breaking Things (Too Much)

Here’s where the real magic happens: the code. In Godot, everything revolves around scenes and nodes, which sounds complicated but actually keeps things organised. I started small, writing scripts to handle Chipper’s movement. Thanks to Godot’s tutorials, I managed to get a smooth jump and double-jump working without tearing my hair out.

Of course, there were bugs. Chipper once launched into orbit after a poorly calculated jump. Another time, he fell straight through the ground. But each bug was an opportunity to learn, and debugging became oddly satisfying. (Pro tip: always save your progress before experimenting with physics.)

## Adding the Finishing Touches

Sound effects and music are the icing on the cake. I created some cheerful chiptunes using a tool called FamiTracker and scoured the internet for free sound effects. Hearing Chipper’s little "boop" every time he jumped was deeply satisfying.

Finally, I added a title screen with a big, bold logo that screams "90s!" and a level select menu—because what’s a retro platformer without one?

## Testing and Sharing the Joy

Once the game was playable (if not entirely polished), I handed it over to a few friends for testing. Their feedback was invaluable: "The vending machine boss is too hard!" and "Can Chipper have a hat?" were just some of the gems. Tweaking the game based on their suggestions was all part of the fun.

Finally, I packaged up the game and shared it online, hoping it would bring a smile to fellow fans of 90s platformers. And honestly? Seeing someone enjoy something you created is one of the best feelings in the world.

## You Can Do It, Too!

Creating a game in Godot isn’t just doable—it’s downright delightful. Whether you’re a coding wizard or a total newbie, the engine makes it easy to bring your ideas to life. And if, like me, you’re driven by a love for the 90s, you might just find yourself creating something that feels like it belongs on a CRT TV next to your SNES.

So, dust off your nostalgia, grab Godot, and start building your dream game. Who knows? Your plucky little robot might just be the next retro gaming hero.
